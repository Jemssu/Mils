The Unlearning: A Conversion Story

The certificates and medals, enclosed in a Durabox at the back of my room, still feel familiar to the touch—the certificates' rigid yet smooth texture and the medals' glossy, firm feel. For ten years, those were what adorned me. My identity was a collection of red ink on papers and numbers on cards, a run of a dream with a single goal: standing on the school stage, with the speaker system calling my name alongside the list of awards I received. I lived in a world of physical perfection where anything less than a 90 felt like a failure of character. I was, in my own mind, bulletproof and destined for glory.

My "cannonball" arrived on a windy Friday at Martin Hall at the end of the first semester in my sophomore year. It marked the fifth day of an illness I wasn't aware of. During my PE class, I lay on the floor, seeking the cold comfort it offered, easing my rising body temperature. I knew I was unwell since Monday, but as I joined an inter-school competition that same week, I pushed myself through. Friday came, and I eventually decided to get a blood test and a check-up. The doctor said it was dengue and told me to "just keep yourself hydrated and you'll be fine." Two days later, I was rushed to the ER with my sickly body unable to hold itself together. Five days in the hospital, for the first time in my life, I had a seizure. In these fifteen minutes, this viral infection swiftly took my brain with ease, shattering the trajectory of my life.

The weeks that followed were a grueling convalescence, not just of my health, but of my pride. With my deteriorated ability to walk, write, and speak, I was bedridden—trapped in the endless cycle of reverting to being a child: holding walls to walk, squeezing objects to regain mobility, and reading children's books out loud to practice. While the world moved on without me, I felt my sense of self-worth dissolve. I spent hours looking over my old certificates, trying to find a way back to who I was, only to realize that the more I clung to past glory, the more empty I became.

This hollowness eventually led me to make a choice, as my dad had requested: to give up my academic honor for my health. I began the second semester with a heart full of self-disappointment, feeling out of place amongst my classmates. For months, I sat in the middle of the classroom, questioning the finality of my decision. It wasn't a sudden unfolding of peace, but of stripping away layers of who I thought I had to be. I was finally finding a sense of purpose, not in a certificate or a medal, but in the uncomfortable yet graceful resolution of simply choosing myself, my happiness.

At long last, I understood that my life's race wasn't meant to be spent glued to my laptop screen, notebooks out with pens and highlighters sprawled across my desk. It was meant to be experienced in the mundane days I spend with my classmates, in the laughter I share with my friends, and in the peace I find with my God. I haven't opened the Durabox in over a year. I don't need the sense of falsified purpose anymore. As I put down my pen, I traded my urgent, desperate pursuit for honor for a slow, steady pace of happiness and serenity with my loved ones and God.
